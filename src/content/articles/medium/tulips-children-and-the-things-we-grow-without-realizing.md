Last winter, a friend handed me a bag of tulip bulbs and said, _“Just stick them in the dirt and forget about them.”_ I hesitated. Shouldn’t something so beautiful need constant care? Fertilizer, precise watering, a pep talk now and then? But I buried them anyway, half-expecting failure.

I’m used to thinking anything worth having needs hustle, support, control.<br>But months later, those tulips pushed through cold soil and bloomed — all without me lifting a finger. No instruction manual. No begging for attention. Just raw, stubborn life doing what it’s wired to do.

It got me thinking: We overcomplicate growth. Especially when it comes to children.

## The Myth of Control

We’ve been sold a lie: that anything worthwhile requires backbreaking effort, color-coded planners, and a five-year strategy.

But look at nature — tulips don’t stress about blooming. Kids don’t need PowerPoints on how to walk. They stumble, faceplant, and still get up laughing.

Meanwhile, we’re over here treating parenthood like a spreadsheet, terrified we’ll mess up because we forgot to optimize naptime or buy the _right_ organic milk.

Here’s the truth:

Children grow _despite_ you, not because of you. You think they’ll listen?

Please. Did _you_?

Yet here you are — alive, functional, maybe even happy.

They grow from presence, not pressure.<br>From environment, not instructions.

Most of us became who we are _in spite_ of what we were told, not because of it.

## The Myth of “It’ll Ruin My Life”

A lot of people my age hesitate about having children.<br>They say it’s about money, freedom, career goals, responsibilities — and fair enough, those are real concerns.

But under all that, I think it’s fear of _change_.<br>Fear that life will become harder, more chaotic, more “not about you.”

Here’s the truth:<br>Yes, it will be harder.<br>But also, it’ll make you more capable.<br>And oddly enough, more _yourself_.

Responsibility has a way of clearing the noise.<br>You stop wasting time.<br>You stop spiraling on problems because, frankly, you don’t have the luxury to overthink anymore. You just figure it out.

When you _have_ to show up — for a kid, a garden, a life — you stop wasting energy on _“What if…?”_ and start figuring out _“How?”_

## The Consequence of Believing On Every Thought

The mind is a strange thing.<br>It can imagine the best future — and still choose to obsess over the worst one.

If all you picture is bills, burnout, and broken dreams, then that’s all you’ll notice.<br>It’s like tuning your mental radio to the “Everything Will Go Wrong” channel.

But shift the station slightly — <br>What if you imagined a future where you could _actually_ do both?<br>Build your dreams _and_ raise someone who dreams freely because you did?

That’s not wishful thinking.<br>That’s practical optimism.

If you fixate on _“Kids will ruin my dreams,”_ your brain will serve up proof: sleepless nights, messy floors, sacrifices. But if you dwell on _“Kids will force me to grow,”_ suddenly you see solutions: remote work, shared chores, a legacy that outlives you.

## Let Me Be Honest About My Own Chaos

Last year, I hit a wall.

The return-to-office rule drained me. I wasn’t eating well, moving much, or sleeping right.<br>I felt off — so I started looking for answers.

I spent a year obsessing over Purpose → Philosophy → Psychology → Neuroscience → Science → Physics → Math → Spirituality → Yoga, only to realize I’d skipped the basics: Eat better. Move. Sleep. The answers were always there; I just preferred the drama of searching.

Still, that journey taught me how my mind works.<br>And how _direction matters more than speed_.

The more I learned, the better my imagination got.<br>I could see bigger dreams — and weirdly, I felt more willing to work for them.<br>Because now I believed they were actually possible.

## Kids Don’t Need Perfect Parents. Just Real Ones.

You don’t have to be flawless.<br>You just have to be someone your kid can observe and learn from.

They’re not listening to your lectures. They’re watching how you live.<br>Your habits become their baseline.<br>Your moods become their weather.

So if your own life is somewhat sorted — if you eat decently, move your body, care about things — they’ll soak that in.<br>And in the process, your life might actually improve too.<br>It’s hard to slack off when someone’s always watching.

## We Don’t Need to Add to Life. Just Stop Avoiding It.

You were born with everything you need:<br>A body that works. A brain that imagines. A world full of food, water, and people.

But we’ve been sold this idea that something’s missing.<br>That unless you achieve X or own Y, you’re incomplete.<br>It’s not true.

The only real difference between us and other animals is: we can imagine.<br>We can picture what tomorrow could be like — then walk toward it.

So maybe don’t imagine the worst.<br>Don’t write off something just because a reel or a rant made it sound like hell.

Try things for yourself.<br>Let life surprise you.

## Final Thought

A tulip doesn’t ask if the timing’s right.<br>It grows when the conditions are _just enough_.<br>Not perfect. Just enough.

You might be just enough right now.

And if you’re unsure — that’s okay.<br>But make sure the story you’re telling yourself isn’t smaller than the life you’re capable of growing.

Start there.<br>Plant something.<br>Let it surprise you.
