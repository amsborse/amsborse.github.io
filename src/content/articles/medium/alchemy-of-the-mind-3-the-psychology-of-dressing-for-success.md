Two weeks ago, I found myself standing at the threshold of my bedroom, freshly changed into my favorite pajamas. It was one of those evenings where I could practically feel the bed calling my name — just one more moment of sweet, uninterrupted rest. But then, as I was about to collapse onto the soft, inviting sheets, awareness struck me like a lightning bolt: If I lie down now, I’ll never get anything done.

Why was it that the moment I decided to be productive, I found myself facing this overwhelming pull toward the comfort of my bed? A deeper question nagged at me — what happened between my initial burst of ambition and the inevitable slide into the comforts of sloth? I needed a way to resist the siren call of my sheets. So, naturally, I came up with a brilliant plan: I’ll wear something nice. Because, clearly, the thought of ruining a sharp outfit with bed head and sleepy drool would be enough to keep me away from the bed, right? Logical, right? (Cue sarcasm🙂.)

But as I stood there, patting myself on the back for this stroke of genius, I realized something: I was hardly the first person to come up with this trick. In fact, I wasn’t even close to being the inventor. Turns out, there’s a fancy term for this — [**enclothed cognition**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclothed_cognition). Who knew? It’s this psychological mechanism where the clothes you wear influence your thoughts and actions, making you more productive, confident, or even alert. This wasn’t just my hack, it’s science.

The term was coined by Hajo Adam and Adam D. Galinsky in 2012, after they tested the impact of white lab coats. Their experiment showed that wearing a lab coat made people more focused and smarter — because, as it turns out, wearing a lab coat triggers the brain to tap into the abstract concepts of professionalism and intellect.

So, while I was busy congratulating myself for my ‘brilliant’ trick to stay out of bed, it turns out I was just tapping into an age-old cognitive hack that many others have used to stay sharp, productive, and well, not face down in their pillow.

## The Office Clothes Hack

You come home from work, exhausted. The first instinct? Change into home clothes, sink into the couch, and promise yourself you’ll be productive “in a bit.” Spoiler: **that bit never comes.**

Here’s the trick: **don’t change into home clothes.** Stay in your office attire. That discomfort? That slight reluctance to lie down? That’s your edge. It keeps you in **work mode**, stopping you from slipping into full relaxation before you’ve done something for yourself — be it writing, side projects, or learning a new skill.

“But my office clothes will get ruined!” some might protest. Well, what’s the bigger risk — your clothes or your productivity?

If you must change, swap only into **exercise clothes**. This keeps momentum alive — either you get moving or get to work, but you don’t sink into idleness.

## The Work-From-Home Formula

For remote workers, **dressing up is a mental switch**. Rolling out of bed and straight into work in pajamas blurs the lines between “home” and “work,” making focus a constant struggle. The solution? **Dress for work, even at home.**

When you glance in the mirror and see yourself in work clothes, your brain gets the message: **this is work mode.** You’re less likely to take meetings from bed or half-read documentation while scrolling on your phone. Dressing up creates an environment where work feels real — because it is.

## The Science of Dressing Up

Psychologists call this _enclothed cognition_ — your clothes don’t just shape how others see you; they shape how you see yourself. Wearing structured, professional clothing enhances cognitive processing and confidence. Athletes wear performance gear for a reason. You, too, can hack your mindset by simply changing what you wear.

Your brain takes cues from your environment. Make sure it’s getting the right signals.

**Now, before you close this tab — what are you wearing right now? And is it helping you become who you want to be?**
